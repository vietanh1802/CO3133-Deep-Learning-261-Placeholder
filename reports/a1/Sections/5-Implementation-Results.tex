\section{Implementation results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Test performance and model size}
\label{sec:quantitative}
The main comparison uses Fashion-MNIST. Each of the six architectures was trained
for 50 epochs with seed 42, the shared split and settings in
Section~\ref{sec:methods}, and one configuration per model. The checkpoint with
the lowest validation cross-entropy supplies predictions on all 10,000
unaugmented official test images. The results use the saved CUDA test records in
\codepath{results/a1/<dataset>_<model>_seed42/}; preliminary five-epoch runs and
earlier Colab outputs are excluded. MNIST development results and the optional
CIFAR-10 extension are reported separately in Section~\ref{sec:additional-results}.

\input{Tables/comparison}

Transformer has the highest observed Fashion-MNIST accuracy, 90.43\%, and
macro-F1, 90.40\%. GRU follows at 89.88\% accuracy and 89.90\% macro-F1, with
LSTM at 89.52\% for both rounded metrics. The 0.55-percentage-point accuracy
difference between Transformer and GRU corresponds to 55 images in this test
set. LSTM makes 36 more errors than GRU, a difference of 0.36 percentage points.
These are measured differences from one training seed, not estimates of
variation across repeated runs.

The MLP improves on the linear classifier by 10.13 percentage points in accuracy,
from 77.05\% to 87.18\%. CNN achieves 86.21\%, 0.97 percentage points below MLP,
while using 19,562 rather than 235,146 trainable parameters. Thus, convolution
alone does not make this CNN configuration superior on Fashion-MNIST. Accuracy
and macro-F1 give the same ordering here, but neither reveals the class-specific
differences examined in Section~\ref{sec:errors}.

\subsection{Learning behavior}
\label{sec:learning-results}
Figure~\ref{fig:fashion-learning} compares the complete 50-epoch loss histories.
The linear classifier improves early, then remains near a training loss of
0.77; its minimum validation loss is 0.6540 at epoch 14. The MLP continues to
improve, ending at training loss 0.4388 and reaching minimum validation loss
0.3413 at epoch 49. These histories and test results are consistent with
limited performance from the affine pixel baseline under the present settings.
They do not establish that a different schedule could not improve it.

GRU, LSTM, and Transformer reach lower validation losses than MLP and CNN in
these runs. Transformer reaches 0.2630 at epoch 44, GRU 0.2676 at epoch 49,
and LSTM 0.2754 at epoch 46. LSTM training loss decreases from 0.9785 to
0.2732 over the run, whereas validation loss ends at 0.2905, above its selected
minimum. Later training updates do not guarantee improved validation performance.

CNN validation loss fluctuates more strongly, with a minimum of 0.3597 at
epoch 48 and a final value of 0.4841. Selecting the final epoch would discard
the better validation checkpoint. This late deterioration motivates inspecting
training stability, but the experiment does not isolate its cause. Training
augmentation, dropout, and batch normalization differ from validation conditions,
so the sign of a train--validation loss gap alone is insufficient to diagnose
overfitting. All runs finish their 50-epoch budget; checkpoint selection is
distinct from early stopping.

\subsection{Per-class performance and error analysis}
\label{sec:errors}
The official Fashion-MNIST test set contains 1,000 images per class.
Table~\ref{tab:class-recall} reports recall for all six models. For detailed
comparison, we select MLP as the flattened-pixel baseline because its minimum
validation loss is lower than Linear's, 0.3413 against 0.6540. Test accuracy
does not determine this baseline choice.

\input{Tables/error_recall}

\paragraph{Distribution of errors.}
Shirt has the lowest recall for every model. Its recall rises from 40.3\% for
Linear to 62.1\% for MLP, 72.4\% for GRU, and 72.3\% for Transformer. CNN
attains 59.6\% and LSTM 70.9\%. In contrast, Bag recall lies between 97.1\%
and 98.7\% for the five nonlinear models. Even the higher-scoring architectures
retain a substantial weakness on Shirt.

Figure~\ref{fig:paired-confusion} shows directional errors for MLP and CNN.
Of the 1,000 Shirt images, MLP assigns 179 to T-shirt/top and CNN assigns 191.
CNN assigns 175 Coat images to Pullover and 112 to Shirt, achieving 67.8\%
Coat recall. MLP assigns 96 Coat images to Pullover and achieves 79.7\%
Coat recall. CNN improves Pullover recall from 79.8\% to 86.3\%, but reduces
Coat recall by 11.9 percentage points. Its effect is class-dependent.

Errors within the group T-shirt/top, Pullover, Coat, and Shirt account for

garment outlines offer a plausible explanation for this concentration,
consistent with the class-average similarities in Section~\ref{sec:eda}.
The experiment does not establish that flattening or any particular
architectural component causes these confusions.

\subsection{Predictions on shared images}
\label{sec:qualitative}
Across the complete test set, CNN correctly classifies 532 images that MLP
misclassifies. Conversely, MLP correctly classifies 629 images that CNN
misclassifies. Both models err on 750 images and correctly classify 8,089.
The disagreement counts give CNN 97 additional errors, matching its
0.97-percentage-point accuracy deficit. Neither model's correct predictions
contain all those of the other.

Figure~\ref{fig:shared-predictions} illustrates the three error categories.
Within each category, we take the first two examples in official test-set
order, using zero-based indices. This deterministic rule fixes the examples,
but their equal number per category does not represent relative frequencies.
Predictions from all six models are shown for each image.

For Coat image 51, Linear, MLP, and LSTM predict Pullover, while CNN, GRU,
and Transformer predict Coat. For Coat image 6, however, MLP and LSTM are
correct and CNN predicts Pullover. The same class pair contributes errors
in both disagreement directions. Image 17 provides another distinction:
MLP and CNN predict Shirt for a Coat that GRU, LSTM, and Transformer classify
correctly. For image 23, only Linear identifies Ankle boot; all five nonlinear
models predict Sandal. These examples demonstrate differences in individual
predictions without establishing general superiority of an architecture.

\subsection{Architecture trade-offs}
\label{sec:discussion}
For accuracy and model size, GRU provides a useful compromise in the main
comparison. Transformer uses 1,191,050 parameters against GRU's 61,962,
approximately 19.2 times as many, for a 0.55-percentage-point accuracy gain.
LSTM uses 82,186 parameters, 32.6\% more than GRU, and has slightly lower
Fashion-MNIST accuracy in this run. CNN is smaller still, but its lower
accuracy and Coat/Shirt errors limit the benefit of that reduction.

The representations offer possible explanations for these patterns. MLP
introduces nonlinear pixel interactions. CNN shares local filters, while global
average pooling removes explicit spatial coordinates from the final features.
GRU and LSTM aggregate rows in order; Transformer connects row positions
through attention. These are structural differences, not isolated experimental
treatments. Capacity, regularization, and the chosen configuration also differ,
so the results do not show that one inductive bias is universally better.

\paragraph{Recorded computation cost.}
Table~\ref{tab:comparison} retains training durations from the saved summaries.
The measured fit interval covers the 50-epoch training/validation loop and
checkpoint writes, excluding initial data/model preparation and final plotting.
For example, LSTM records 620.1 s, GRU 652.5 s, and Transformer 685.0 s.
The corresponding CUDA inference records are 0.230 s, 0.310 s, and 0.269 s
for all 10,000 test images, respectively.

All current test records identify the NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Laptop GPU,
Linux, Python 3.12.14, and PyTorch 2.14.0+cu126. Inference time sums synchronized
forward passes in evaluation mode with batch size 128; data loading, host-to-device
input transfer, softmax, metric computation, and figure generation are excluded.
No separate warm-up or repeated timing trials are recorded. Older non-LSTM
training logs identify CUDA but do not preserve the GPU model, while LSTM also
has an explicit run-environment record. Thus, these durations document executions
and should not be interpreted as a reliable hardware-controlled speed ranking.
Parameter count alone does not specify latency.

\subsection{Development results and optional extension}
\label{sec:additional-results}
\input{Tables/additional_results}

\paragraph{MNIST development runs.}
MNIST is retained as a development check rather than the primary benchmark.
Transformer reaches 99.31\% test accuracy, LSTM 99.27\%, and GRU 99.24\%.
The Transformer--LSTM difference is four of 10,000 images, and LSTM--GRU
is three images. With one seed, these small differences provide no evidence
of a stable ordering across repeated training runs. Figure~\ref{fig:additional-learning}
shows that the nonlinear models reach much lower losses than Linear.

\paragraph{CIFAR-10 extension.}
Under the same epoch budget, CNN reaches 63.84\% accuracy versus MLP's 47.67\%,
a gain of 16.17 percentage points. CNN uses 20,138 parameters compared with
820,874 for MLP. This reverses the MLP--CNN ordering on Fashion-MNIST and
shows that the observed trade-off depends on the dataset and configuration.
LSTM reaches 64.42\%, slightly above GRU's 64.24\%; Transformer reaches
68.71\% accuracy and 68.64\% macro-F1. The recurrent models do not have a
consistent ordering across the three datasets.

\subsection{Limitations}
Only one seed and one configuration per model are available. There are no
across-run uncertainty estimates or significance tests. Equal epochs and
optimizer settings do not match parameter capacity, regularization, or total
compute, and no systematic hyperparameter search was conducted. The experiment
compares complete configurations rather than isolating the causal effect of
architectural components.

The synchronized CUDA evaluations make the current test environment explicit,
but single-pass inference measurements lack warm-up and repeated trials.
Incomplete hardware records for older training runs limit interpretation of
training-time differences. Saved evaluation revisions are marked as dirty
working trees, so a commit hash alone does not identify every source change;
configurations, checkpoints, and prediction evidence must be retained together.

The qualitative analysis concentrates on Fashion-MNIST, and selected examples
are not a random sample of failures. Class-average similarity and individual
errors motivate explanations but do not verify their causes. Neither robustness
to distribution shift nor confidence calibration was tested. These small,
fixed-category datasets do not establish performance on unfamiliar clothing,
natural scenes, or deployment data.

\subsection{Conclusion}
For the tested Fashion-MNIST configurations, Transformer achieves the highest
accuracy and macro-F1, while GRU gives a smaller model with a modest reduction
in accuracy. LSTM is close to GRU, and their ranking changes with the dataset.
CNN offers a compact representation, but its benefits are class- and
dataset-dependent. Across architectures, similar upper-body garments remain
a major source of mistakes, so aggregate scores must be read alongside class
recall and shared-image predictions.

A useful next experiment is a controlled comparison of row-sequence and spatial
representations across several seeds, with an explicit parameter or compute
budget. Repeating the timings after warm-up would separately test execution
cost. These follow-ups would assess whether the observed accuracy, size, and
error trade-offs persist beyond the present runs.
